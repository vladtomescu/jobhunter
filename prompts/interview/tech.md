# Tech round guide

The round where engineers check that I can do the work. It comes in several formats, and each one needs different prep.

## Who is in the room

One or two engineers from the team, often a senior engineer or the tech lead.

## Formats

1. Live algorithm coding: one or two problems solved in a shared editor or on a coding platform, 45 to 60 minutes.
2. Pair programming on a realistic problem: a feature or a bug in a small codebase, 60 to 90 minutes, sometimes with AI tools allowed.
3. A take-home with a live review, in which every decision is defended. Take-homes are trusted less now that AI writes them, so the review probes why each choice was made.
4. Questions on the stack: language and runtime fundamentals, data access, testing, CI/CD, design patterns.
5. A code review or refactoring exercise on code they bring.

The rules on AI tools split between companies. Canva has run an AI-assisted coding round since June 2025 and expects candidates to use Copilot, Cursor or Claude; Amazon may disqualify a candidate for using AI; Meta is testing AI-enabled interviews. Never assume either way.

When the context does not name the format, cover the formats the company is reported to use, and give me the one question to ask the recruiter before the call: what the format is, which language and editor, whether AI tools are allowed, how long it runs and who will be there.

## What they score

- Working, correct code in the coding formats.
- The habits, in every format: clarify the input, state the assumptions, think aloud, give the complexity without being asked, and test with cases, the edge cases included.
- Depth on the stack: how things work underneath, not only how to use them.
- Code quality: naming, structure, tests, error handling.
- How I take a hint or a correction.

## Format and length

45 to 90 minutes by video, with a shared editor or a shared screen. See Formats.

## Likely questions, ranked

By format:

1. Stack questions, from the technologies in the posting and in my files: the runtime and memory, concurrency, the database and transactions, the testing strategy, the CI/CD pipeline, a design pattern I use and why.
2. Live coding: a medium problem on arrays, strings, maps, trees, graphs or intervals, then a follow-up to optimize or extend it.
3. Pair programming: a small feature or a bug fix in a realistic codebase, with tests.
4. Take-home review: why this structure, how it was tested, what breaks at ten times the load, what I would change with more time.
5. Code review: what I would flag, in what order, and how I would say it to the author.

In every format: a hard bug or a production incident, and how I found the cause.

## What to prepare

- Pitch: the 30-second version.
- Reference systems: the deep-dive points and the honest limits.
- Gaps and bridges: one for every technology in the posting I lack.
- Drills: the five habits, practised aloud on a timer.
- Stories: a hard bug or a production incident.
- For a take-home: every decision, and what I would change.

## Cards

Only my own facts and a checklist, nothing to read out: my systems (the components, versions, dates, the choices and why; a design target only when labelled as a target); the habits (clarify, assumptions aloud, think aloud, complexity, test); the format question for the recruiter. The subtitle for this round carries "think aloud" and "share one window". My questions last: how the team reviews code, how it tests and ships, the next steps.

## Mock interviewer

- Persona: a senior engineer on the team.
- Behaviour: friendly but exact. Asks why after each decision. Gives a hint only after I have been stuck for a while.
- Question plan, by format: for stack questions, six to eight questions by voice, from the stack in the posting and my files; for live coding, one medium problem run as a text chat in which I paste my code, then one follow-up to optimize or extend it; for a take-home review, the questions above about my own solution.
- Press on: the reason for each decision, edge cases, the complexity, how I would test it, what I would do differently.
- Level: a senior engineer working in the company's stack.
- Clock: about 30 minutes for stack questions by voice; 25 to 35 minutes for a coding problem.
- Ending: time for my questions, the next steps, goodbye.

## Feedback focus

- The five habits, one by one.
- Correctness, and the complexity stated without being asked.
- Communication while coding: did I explain before typing?
- Gaps stated honestly, with the bridge.
- Depth: did the answer go below the surface of the tool?

## Research focus

- The technical format the company is reported to use, and what candidates say they were asked.
- The stack, from the posting, the engineering blog and other job pages of the company.
- Whether the company allows AI tools in its technical rounds.

## Pay

No.
